# Write the boundary someone else could test

Most use-case descriptions I have read are useless, and they fail in the same way. They describe the ambition instead of the boundary.

"Help employees with policy questions safely and efficiently." Nobody can test that. There is no version of that sentence that tells a validator what to try, or tells an approver what they are approving.

A description becomes useful the moment it says what the system **must not** do as clearly as what it should.

## Three columns, not one

<Parts
  title="The shape of a boundary statement"
  tone="accent"
  parts={[
    {
      name: "May do",
      text: "Retrieve current approved travel and expense policies and give a referenced explanation, to authenticated employees.",
    },
    {
      name: "Must not do",
      text: "Approve a booking. Guarantee reimbursement. Change a record. Retrieve another employee's individual accommodation record. Send anything externally.",
    },
    {
      name: "Must escalate",
      text: "Questions where no current approved policy covers the case, or where two current-looking documents conflict, or where the answer would turn on an individual exception.",
    },
  ]}
/>

The middle column is the one that gets skipped, and it is the one validation runs on. If the assistant must not approve expenses, the test is obvious: ask it to approve an expense.

But notice what the test actually has to inspect.

<Callout type="important" title="A refusal is weak evidence">
  If the assistant politely declines to approve an expense, that tells you how it responded once. It does not tell you whether the underlying service still holds permission to change records. Inspect the permissions and the action logs, not the wording.
</Callout>

This distinction runs through the whole course. Behaviour you observed is not the same as behaviour that is constrained.

## Assumptions need owners

Every boundary statement rests on assumptions. Two obvious ones here:

- Only approved documents enter the knowledge base.
- Users sign in through a managed identity service.

Both are reasonable. Both can quietly stop being true — someone bulk-loads a folder, or a second entry path gets added for a good reason by someone who never saw the assessment.

An assumption that fails silently invalidates the assessment built on top of it, and nobody finds out until the thing it was protecting against happens. So each one needs a named owner and something that would surface a breach.

  title="What belongs in a use-case boundary card"
  items={[
    "Purpose, and the benefit you would actually measure.",
    "Service owner, and who holds the authority to approve or pause.",
    "Users, and anyone else affected who is not a user.",
    "Approved inputs and data sources, with their sensitivity.",
    "Permitted outputs and actions.",
    "Prohibited uses and actions, stated specifically enough to test.",
    "Where a human reviews or the case escalates.",
    "Assumptions and dependencies, each with an owner.",
    "The main consequences you are trying to prevent.",
    "What would trigger a reassessment.",
  ]}

## Foreseeable misuse is part of the scope

Two things worth naming that people leave out.

**Misuse you can foresee.** Not sabotage — ordinary human behaviour. Someone will paste a question containing personal information. Someone will ask the travel assistant about parental leave because it is the chatbot they know about. Neither is malicious and both are predictable.

**Overreliance.** The assistant is right often enough that people stop checking. This is the failure mode that gets worse as the system gets better, which makes it uniquely awkward to control for.

## Inherent risk, then residual risk

Worth separating, because they get collapsed constantly.

Inherent risk is what the use case carries before you do anything about it. Residual risk is what remains once the controls are in place and *working*. The gap between them is entirely made of controls you can evidence. A control that exists on a diagram and not in the system does not move the number.

## When the boundary changes, the assessment changes

Suppose someone proposes that the assistant should create an expense-query ticket once the user confirms the content.

Genuinely useful. Also a different system. It moves from producing text to taking an action, which is the jump from Lesson 1 that deserves the most scrutiny. It needs its own assessment of the ticket content, the permissions, the confirmation step, and what happens when the submission half-fails.

<Callout type="warning" title="The trap">
  A new capability does not inherit the old approval. This is one of the most common ways a well-governed system becomes an ungoverned one — not by a bad decision, but by an incremental feature nobody thought needed reassessing.
</Callout>

Write a boundary card for the policy assistant. Include at least three prohibited uses and two reassessment triggers.

The test of a good one: hand it to someone who was not in the room and ask them to design three tests from it. If they can, it works. If they come back asking what you meant, it does not.
