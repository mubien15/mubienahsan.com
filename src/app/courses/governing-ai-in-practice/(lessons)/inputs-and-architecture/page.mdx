# Validate the system, not the model

A model benchmark cannot tell you whether your policy assistant retrieved the right document for the right employee. It was never measuring that.

This is the most common category error in AI assurance. Someone asks "is the model any good?", gets a credible answer, and treats the question as settled. But the model is one component among several, and most of the failures we care about live in the others.

## What the application actually is

Walk the path a question takes:

1. A user arrives, and identity and access controls determine who they are and what they may see.
2. Documents were collected, prepared and indexed at some earlier point, by some process, with some rules about retirement.
3. A retrieval service selects passages it judges relevant.
4. Instructions tell the model how to use those passages.
5. The model generates an answer.
6. The interface presents it, with or without the source, the date, and the caveats.

Six stages. The model is one of them. Every other stage can produce a wrong answer without the model doing anything unusual at all.

<Callout type="important" title="The example that makes it concrete">
  The model correctly and faithfully summarises the document it was given. The document is the retired policy with the 150-unit cap. The answer is perfectly grounded in its source and completely wrong for the question asked. No model improvement fixes this.
</Callout>

## Four areas to review

A practical way to scope validation, borrowed from how model review already works and stretched to fit these systems.

### 1. Use and context

Start where Lesson 2 finished: purpose, users, permitted and prohibited actions, autonomy, assumptions. If the boundary is vague, everything downstream is unfalsifiable.

### 2. Inputs and architecture

<Checklist
  title="Questions for the sources"
  items={[
    "Are the documents approved, and by whom?",
    "Are they current, and how would you know if one were not?",
    "How are retired documents handled — removed, marked, or quietly left in place?",
    "How long does a change take to become visible to the assistant?",
    "Does every source have an owner who would notice if it went stale?",
  ]}
/>

Then retrieval itself. A correct answer is difficult if the system never finds the relevant policy, so test whether it does. And test permissions separately: **a document must not become available to someone merely because its text is a good match for their question.** Relevance and authorisation are different questions, and a retrieval system that conflates them is a disclosure waiting to happen.

Prompts and configuration need versions. If you cannot say which prompt produced a given answer, you cannot reproduce anything.

### 3. Implementation and outputs

The next lesson is entirely about this, so we will leave it there.

### 4. Weaknesses and operating conditions

What the system is bad at, under what conditions, and what restrictions follow from that. This section is the one people leave thin, and it is the one a reader actually needs.

## Third-party models

You will usually be deploying someone else's model, and you will not get the training data.

Two things follow. First, gather what evidence is available — capability documentation, stated limitations, evaluation results, known failure modes — and read it properly rather than filing it. Second, test the configuration you actually deploy, because the provider's evaluation was not run on your prompts, your documents, or your users.

<Callout type="warning" title="An evidence limitation is not an assumption of quality">
  Not being able to inspect training data is a real constraint, and it should be written down as one. It is not a reason to assume the thing is fine. State what you could not examine and what that means for your conclusion.
</Callout>

## The unit of validation

The system in its operating context. Not the model. Not the prompt. Not the retrieval service in isolation.

A failure may come from the model, the data, an integration, the permissions, or the way a user interprets what they are shown. If your validation only covers one of those, your conclusion only covers one of those — and you should say so in the report rather than let the reader assume otherwise.
