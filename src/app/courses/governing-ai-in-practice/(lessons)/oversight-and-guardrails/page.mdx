# Controls, and the limits of human review

"A human will check it" is the most common control description in AI governance and one of the least informative.

It does not say who. It does not say what they can see. It does not say how long they have, how many of these they do an hour, or whether they have the authority to say no. Without those, it is a hope with a job title attached.

Two well-documented effects work against all four. **Automation bias**: people defer to a confident machine answer more readily than to a confident human one. **Review fatigue**: the hundredth approval of the day gets less attention than the first, and the failures are not evenly distributed across the day.

Design around this rather than exhorting people to concentrate.

<Callout type="important" title="The test">
  Human involvement is worth something when the human has the information and the authority to exercise judgement. Otherwise you have added a delay and a person to blame.
</Callout>

## Three kinds of control

**Preventive** controls limit what can happen. **Detective** controls tell you something happened. **Corrective** controls contain and repair it. You need all three, and teams reliably over-invest in the second because dashboards are satisfying to build.

## Do not ask the model to enforce your permissions

This is the single most important design rule in the lesson.

If the assistant must not approve an expense, **do not give it an approval tool**. If a user may not see a personnel record, enforce that in the retrieval service against that user's identity — not by instructing the model to decline.

An instruction in a prompt is a request. Everything in Lesson 6 about injection is about what happens when something else in the context makes a competing request. Permissions enforced outside the model are not subject to that argument.

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| Prompt says "never approve expenses" | The service holds no approval credential |
| Prompt says "do not reveal restricted documents" | Retrieval filters by the requesting user's entitlements |
| Model asked to check its own limits | The check runs outside the model, before the action |
| Interface warns the answer may be wrong | Source and version shown next to the answer |

## Controls interact

A reviewer cannot catch a stale policy if the interface does not show the date. A confirmation button is meaningless if it does not display what is about to be submitted. Least privilege does not help if the agent legitimately holds the tool being misused.

Controls are a system, and their weakest interaction is the real strength.

## Scope oversight to consequence

Not every answer needs review. An ordinary question answered from a current, approved, general policy can go straight to the user — that is the whole point of building the thing.

Route to a human when the case is: missing policy, conflicting sources, an individual exception, or anything the system flags low confidence on. That route needs a named owner and a response time somebody has agreed to, otherwise it is a queue that fills up.

<Callout type="warning" title="Guardrails reduce risk; they do not remove it">
  Filters and refusals are worth having and none of them are guarantees. Write your controls as reducing the likelihood or the impact of a specific failure, and resist any description that implies the failure has been eliminated.
</Callout>

## The hypothetical next phase

If ticket creation is ever approved, the controls around it look like this: show the destination and the exact proposed content, require explicit confirmation, enforce the allowed fields server-side rather than in the prompt, and make duplicate submission impossible.

Note that three of those four are outside the model.
