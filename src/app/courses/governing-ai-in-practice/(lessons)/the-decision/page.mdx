# The decision, and defending it

You now have the pieces: a defined purpose, accountable roles, validation evidence, operating controls, and a value case. The last job is to say what the evidence justifies.

Not "the AI is approved". That sentence cannot be defended because it does not specify anything.

## Five options, not two

<Checklist
  title="What a decision can be"
  items={[
    "Approve a defined scope.",
    "Approve with enforceable conditions — conditions somebody can verify, with an owner and a date.",
    "Defer pending specific remediation.",
    "Restrict to a narrower scope, population or capability.",
    "Reject, or retire an existing service.",
  ]}

Whichever you pick, record: the rationale, the residual risks, the owner, the conditions, the review date or trigger, and **what the evidence did not cover**. That last item is the one that protects you later.

## The capstone

Here is the evidence. All figures fictional.

| Evidence | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Answer quality | 100 curated questions; 94 judged acceptable. Of the 6: four relied on retired policy, two added unsupported claims |
| Authorisation test | One synthetic restricted document was exposed to an unauthorised test user |
| Variability | Important questions were not repeated, so stability is uncharacterised |
| Roles | Service owner and content owner assigned; named engineering support exists |
| Recovery | Runbook written; pause and restore never exercised |
| Timing | 8 minutes manual vs 4 assisted, small sample, uncomplicated questions only |
| Proposed next phase | Ticket creation, and access to individual accommodation records. Neither assessed |

Your task: a boundary card, five material risks with owners and controls, six tests, a release decision with reasons, the remediation and evidence required to reconsider, monitoring and reassessment triggers, a qualified reading of the time saving, and a separate decision on the expansion.

## The model answer

**Decision: defer the initial production release.** Keep any further evaluation in a controlled environment with synthetic data.

Three things block it, and the first is decisive on its own.

**The authorisation defect.** A restricted document reached a user who should not have seen it. A 94% answer-quality result does not offset this, because they are not the same question. This is Lesson 6's averaging trap presented as a real decision, and the whole point of the exercise is noticing that the strong number is irrelevant to the blocking finding.

**Retired sources in four of six failures.** That is a pattern, not noise. It points at ingestion and retirement rather than at the model.

**Untested recovery.** A runbook nobody has exercised is a document. You do not know how long a pause takes or what it breaks.

<Callout type="important" title="What reconsideration requires">
  Documented fixes linked to retest results. No observed unauthorised disclosure across a justified access-control evaluation. Current-source tests passing. Repeat-test findings for the high-stakes questions. A successful recovery exercise. And an explicit note that a clean finite test does not prove future disclosure is impossible.
</Callout>

**On the expansion:** assess separately. Ticket creation adds an action capability; individual records add sensitive data. Neither is covered by an approval for advisory answers over general policy, and bundling them into this decision would be the exact mistake Lesson 2 warned about.

**On the timing figure:** promising, preliminary. Describe it as capacity, extend the sample to representative tasks, and include maintenance and support costs.

## Template F — the decision record

Decision date; decision maker and their authority; scope approved or rejected; relevant versions; evidence reviewed; material findings; unresolved uncertainties; decision and rationale; conditions and their owners; restricted capabilities; review trigger or date; monitoring obligations; recovery route; where the record lives.

## How this gets marked

| Criterion | Points | Full credit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope and boundaries | 3 | Users, data, allowed outputs, prohibited actions |
| Accountability | 3 | Operating, oversight and decision roles assigned |
| Risk and controls | 4 | Access, stale content, unsupported output, change, recovery |
| Validation quality | 4 | Observable criteria, versions, severity, repetition, limitations |
| Decision rationale | 4 | Identifies the blockers, specifies evidence to reconsider |
| Value and lifecycle | 2 | Qualifies the benefit, defines triggers |

Pass at 14 of 20 — **and no recommendation to release with the authorisation defect unresolved.** Alternative controls and different role structures are fine if you justify them. That one security condition is not negotiable, because it is the thing the case was built to test.

## The last thing

Your goal was never to promise that failure is impossible. No system clears that bar and any document claiming it should be distrusted on sight.

The goal is to show that the system has a justified purpose, bounded authority, credible evidence, and an operating process that will notice and respond when conditions change. That is a defensible position. "It passed the tests" is not.
