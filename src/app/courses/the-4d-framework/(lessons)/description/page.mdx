# Description: saying what you want

If you have taken my [prompting course](/courses/prompting-basics), this lesson will feel familiar. That is deliberate, and I would rather say so than pretend otherwise. What the framework adds is a sharper cut: it splits describing into three separate things, and once you see the split you notice that almost everyone only ever does one of them.

## The three descriptions

Nearly everyone describes the product. Almost nobody describes the process or the performance, and those two are where the biggest gains hide.

## Product: the one you already do

Even here most people stop too early. "Write me an email" describes a form, not a product.

The second one is not longer for the sake of it. Every clause removes a decision the tool would otherwise make badly on your behalf.

## Process: how to get there

This is the one that surprised me most. You can describe the route, not just the destination.

<PromptToClaude>
  Before you write anything, ask me the five questions you most need answered to do this well.
</PromptToClaude>

That single sentence has saved me more time than any other habit in this course. The questions expose what I had not decided yet. Half the time I realise my own thinking was the thing missing, not the output.

Other process descriptions worth stealing:

- **"Give me three versions with genuinely different approaches, not three rewordings."** Stops you anchoring on the first idea.
- **"Outline it first and wait for me to approve the outline."** Cheap to fix a structure, expensive to fix a finished draft.
- **"Work through this step by step and show your reasoning."** Gives you something to check, which matters enormously for the next lesson.
- **"Argue the opposite case before you answer."** Uncomfortable and useful.

## Performance: how it should behave

The third one is nearly invisible until someone points it out, and then you cannot unsee it.

These tools default to a manner: helpful, agreeable, a bit eager, inclined to give you something rather than admit it does not know. That default is fine for casual use and actively unhelpful when you need a real opinion.

      "An actual opinion, with the weakest point first and the uncertain parts labelled. Occasionally uncomfortable. Considerably more useful.",
  }}
/>

<Callout type="important" title="Why this one matters most">
  A tool that agrees with you is not being nice, it is being useless. If you only ever get encouragement back, you are not getting a second opinion at all, you are getting an echo with better grammar. Describing the performance you want is how you turn it back into a colleague.
</Callout>

## You do not need all three every time

For a quick reformatting job, product alone is plenty. Do not turn this into ceremony.

The three parts are a diagnostic, not a template. When something comes back wrong, ask which of the three you left out. In my experience it is process about half the time, performance about a third, and the rest is genuinely just a hard task.

<Callout type="tip" title="One sentence upgrade">
  If you take one thing from this lesson: add <em>&ldquo;ask me questions first&rdquo;</em> to your next serious request. It is the cheapest description you can add and it does the most work.
</Callout>
